Dolly Parton: Studio Albums Discography

Musicians & Instrumentation (1967–2024)

Dolly Parton was born January 19, 1946, the fourth of twelve children raised in a one-room cabin in Sevier County, Tennessee, without electricity or running water. Her father, Robert Lee Parton, couldn't read or write; her mother's family, the Owenses, carried the musical tradition — hymns, ballads, mountain harmony — that shaped her ear before she ever picked up a guitar. She was singing on local radio and television as a child and signed to Monument Records in 1965, scoring her first chart single with "Dumb Blonde" in 1966. Her debut LP, Hello, I'm Dolly (1967), caught the attention of Porter Wagoner, who hired her to replace Norma Jean on his syndicated television show. What followed was a seven-year professional partnership — his folksy showmanship set against her voice and songwriting — that made her a national name, alongside a prolific run of RCA duet albums recorded in parallel with her solo catalog under staff producer Bob Ferguson.

The Wagoner years produced some of her most enduring writing: Coat of Many Colors (1971) turned childhood poverty into one of country music's most beloved narrative songs, and My Tennessee Mountain Home (1973) mined the same material. But the partnership had grown constricting, and in 1974 she left the show. She said goodbye to Wagoner the only way she knew how — by writing him a song. "I Will Always Love You," cut for the album Jolene (1974), was delivered to him in his office as notice she was leaving; the split still curdled into a 1979 lawsuit over royalties and business dealings, settled out of court. Freed from the arrangement, she produced New Harvest…First Gathering (1977) herself, then handed the reins to pop producer Gary Klein for Here You Come Again (1977), engineered with new manager Sandy Gallin to cross her into the pop charts without losing the steel guitar underneath. It worked: the album went to number one on the country chart and top five on the Hot 100, and the crossover held through Heartbreaker (1978) and into the 1980s, where "9 to 5," her Oscar-nominated theme from her film debut alongside Jane Fonda and Lily Tomlin, made her a movie star as well as a singer. "Islands in the Stream," her signature duet with Kenny Rogers, was written and produced by the Bee Gees but released on Rogers's own album rather than hers — a fact worth knowing even though it sits outside this list.

She left RCA for Columbia in 1987, the same year she joined Emmylou Harris and Linda Ronstadt for Trio, a harmony-singing landmark produced by George Massenburg that won a Grammy and remains one of the most beloved records any of the three ever made; a shelved sequel, Trio II, finally surfaced in 1999. Her solo Columbia run was uneven — Rainbow (1987) flopped, but White Limozeen (1989), produced by bluegrass veteran Ricky Skaggs, restored her to the top of the country charts. The defining artistic turn of her later career came at the end of the 1990s, when she began working with producer Steve Buckingham, first heard on Eagle When She Flies (1991) and deepened through the 1990s, and used it to make a deliberate return to the string-band sound of her childhood: The Grass Is Blue (1999), Little Sparrow (2001), and the self-produced Halos & Horns (2002) form a bluegrass trilogy that critics still consider her most purely satisfying stretch of recording, anchored by a Grammy win for The Grass Is Blue.

From 2008 on she released music through her own label, Dolly Records, in a long-running partnership with producer Kent Wells that carried her through Backwoods Barbie, Better Day, Blue Smoke, and beyond. Her 2022 induction into the Rock & Roll Hall of Fame — which she initially tried to decline, saying she hadn't earned it, before accepting and declaring "I'm a rock star now" — inspired Rockstar (2023), a covers-and-collaborations record that became the biggest chart debut of her career. Her husband of nearly sixty years, Carl Dean, whom she married in secret in 1966 after meeting him outside a Nashville laundromat, died in March 2025 following a long illness. Her final studio release, Smoky Mountain DNA: Family, Faith and Fables (2024), gathered five generations of the Parton and Owens families in a project tracing her music back to its Appalachian roots. Dolly Parton died on August 25, 2026, in Nashville, at 80, after a brief battle with cancer — closing a six-decade catalog that moved from a mountain cabin to Hollywood and back again without ever losing the thread.

Scope: this report covers Parton's solo studio catalog, roughly 40 albums spanning 1967–2024. Excluded: her live albums, compilations, and soundtrack albums; her thirteen duet LPs with Porter Wagoner (1968–1980), a separate discography built around a distinct professional partnership; and songs like "Islands in the Stream," which appear on other artists' records rather than her own. Collaborative studio albums credited to Parton alongside named co-artists — Trio, Trio II, Honky Tonk Angels, and the multi-generational family project Smoky Mountain DNA — are included, since they were released as Dolly Parton albums proper, though Smoky Mountain DNA is a genuine edge case: it is credited to "Dolly Parton & Family" and functions more as a group heritage project than a conventional solo record. A handful of mid-1970s and early-1980s RCA albums carry thin production/personnel documentation in surviving sources; where a detail could not be confirmed, it is marked "details limited" rather than guessed. No posthumous or archival release had been announced as of this writing.

THE MONUMENT & PORTER WAGONER YEARS (1967–1974) Mountain Roots to Nashville Fame

Hello, I'm Dolly
1967		 Singer, co-writer (10 of 12 tracks, with uncle Bill Owens) Fred Foster Monument. Her debut LP, built around the modest hit "Dumb Blonde" (1966); its reception brought her to Porter Wagoner's attention and led directly to her joining his TV show later in 1967.
Just Because I'm a Woman
1968		 Singer, writer Bob Ferguson RCA Victor. Her first RCA solo album after signing that year; the title track pushed back at a double standard toward women's sexual pasts in plain, unapologetic terms.
In the Good Old Days (When Times Were Bad)
1969		 Singer, writer Bob Ferguson RCA Victor. Early set of hardscrabble mountain narratives; establishes the autobiographical vein she'd return to throughout the era.
My Blue Ridge Mountain Boy
1969		 Singer, writer Bob Ferguson RCA Victor. Details limited beyond the Ferguson-produced, mountain-themed content typical of the run.
The Fairest of Them All
1970		 Singer, writer Bob Ferguson RCA Victor. Details limited; personnel not well documented in surviving sources.
The Golden Streets of Glory
1971		 Singer, writer Bob Ferguson RCA Victor. A gospel-leaning record reflecting the church-and-hymn side of her upbringing.
Joshua
1971		 Singer, writer Bob Ferguson RCA Victor. Title song gave her a solo number-one country single, a turning point that made clear she could top the charts without Wagoner.
Coat of Many Colors
1971		 Singer, writer Bob Ferguson RCA Victor. The title track, about a coat her mother sewed from rags and the cruelty of classmates who mocked it, is among the most-loved songs in country music and her own frequent pick as her best work.
Touch Your Woman
1972		 Singer, writer Bob Ferguson RCA Victor. Title track was a top-ten country single; continues the Ferguson-era house sound.
My Favorite Songwriter, Porter Wagoner
1972		 Singer Bob Ferguson RCA Victor. A tribute album of songs written (not sung) by Wagoner — a solo Parton release, not one of the duet LPs, though the title invites confusion.
My Tennessee Mountain Home
1973		 Singer, writer Bob Ferguson RCA Victor. A concept album built entirely around her Sevierville childhood, a companion piece to "Coat of Many Colors."
Bubbling Over
1973		 Singer, writer Bob Ferguson RCA Victor. Details limited; a transitional record as the Wagoner partnership neared its end.
Jolene
1974		 Singer, writer Bob Ferguson RCA Victor. The title song and "I Will Always Love You" (cut June 12, 1973, at RCA Studio B) both appear here; the latter was written as a direct farewell to Wagoner and marks her exit from his show. One of the era's defining albums.
Love Is Like a Butterfly
1974		 Singer, writer Bob Ferguson RCA Victor. The title single was a number-one country hit and closed out the Ferguson/Wagoner-associated run as she stepped fully into a solo career.

The Bargain Store
1975		 Singer, writer Bob Ferguson RCA Victor. Title track's romantic metaphor was blunt enough that some radio stations banned it as suggestive — an early sign of Parton pushing her own material past the label's comfort zone.

THE POP CROSSOVER: GARY KLEIN YEARS (1977–1980) Here You Come Again to Hollywood

Album / YearArtist's RoleProducers & Key MusiciansLabel / Notes
New Harvest…First Gathering
1977		 Singer, writer, producer Dolly Parton (Gregg Perry, 2 tracks) RCA Victor. Her first self-produced album and first aimed squarely at the pop market, cut in both Nashville and Los Angeles. Hit number one on the country albums chart and earned a Grammy nomination for "(Your Love Keeps Lifting Me) Higher and Higher."
Here You Come Again
1977		 Singer Gary Klein RCA Victor. The crossover breakthrough: the Barry Mann/Cynthia Weil title track (written for Brenda Lee, redirected to Parton) went to number one country and number three pop. Klein kept a steel guitar audible in the mix at her insistence. Won the Grammy for Best Female Country Vocal Performance.
Heartbreaker
1978		 Singer Gary Klein RCA Victor. Continued the polished pop-country sound of its predecessor, with the title track another crossover hit.
Great Balls of Fire
1979		 Singer Gary Klein RCA Victor. Closed out the run of Klein-produced pop-leaning albums heading into the new decade.
Dolly, Dolly, Dolly
1980		 Singer Details limited RCA Victor. A commercially softer, transitional record between the Klein pop run and her film-driven work later in the year.
9 to 5 and Odd Jobs
1980		 Singer, writer Mike Post ("9 to 5" produced by Gregg Perry) RCA Victor. Tied to her film debut in 9 to 5 alongside Jane Fonda and Lily Tomlin. The Oscar-nominated title song hit number one country and pop; the rest of the album mixed originals with folk and country standards by Woody Guthrie, Mel Tillis, and Merle Travis.

Heartbreak Express
1982		 Singer, writer Details limited RCA Victor. Released the same year as her film The Best Little Whorehouse in Texas; her Hollywood career was now running parallel to her recording career.
Burlap & Satin
1983		 Singer, writer, co-producer Dolly Parton & Gregg Perry RCA Victor. Released the same year as "Islands in the Stream," her signature duet with Kenny Rogers — written and produced by the Bee Gees but issued on Rogers's own album, Eyes That See in the Dark, not this one.

Real Love
1985		 Singer, writer David Malloy RCA Victor. Her last studio album for RCA after nearly twenty years with the label. Title track, a duet with Kenny Rogers, and "Think About Love" both hit number one country.

COLUMBIA YEARS: TRIO & ROOTS REVIVAL (1987–1993) Harmony Singing, Comeback, Duets

Trio
1987Dolly Parton, Emmylou Harris & Linda Ronstadt		 Singer, co-writer George Massenburg; David Lindley, Mark O'Connor Warner Bros. A harmony-singing landmark; went platinum and roughly four million worldwide, won the Grammy for Best Country Performance by a Duo or Group, and was nominated for Album of the Year.
Rainbow
1987		 Singer, executive producer Steve "Gold-E" Goldstein Columbia. Her first solo album after leaving RCA for Columbia; a pop-leaning record, including a duet with Smokey Robinson, that sold poorly (peaking at #153) and scuttled Columbia's original plan to alternate pop and country releases.
White Limozeen
1989		 Singer, writer Ricky Skaggs Columbia. A commercial comeback after Rainbow: RIAA Gold, two number-one country singles ("Why'd You Come in Here Lookin' Like That," "Yellow Roses"), plus a bluegrass-flavored cover of REO Speedwagon's "Time for Me to Fly."
Home for Christmas
1990		 Singer Details limited Columbia. Her second Christmas album.
Eagle When She Flies
1991		 Singer, writer, executive producer Steve Buckingham & Gary Smith Columbia. RIAA Platinum with a strong opening week; the first collaboration with Steve Buckingham, whose partnership with Parton would deepen through the decade and shape her later bluegrass turn.
Slow Dancing with the Moon
1993		 Singer, writer Steve Buckingham Columbia. Continued the Buckingham-produced Columbia sound heading into her all-star duets record the same year.
Honky Tonk Angels
1993Dolly Parton, Loretta Lynn & Tammy Wynette		 Singer, co-producer Steve Buckingham (co-produced with Parton) Columbia. Billboard called the trio "three country cornerstones" on release; mostly standards, plus originals from all three singers. Certified Gold.
