Miles Davis: Studio Albums as Leader

Musicians & Key Personnel (1951–1991)

Scope: This report covers original studio albums led by Miles Davis. It excludes live albums, compilations, co-billed collaborations (e.g. Miles Davis and Milt Jackson, Blue Moods on Debut), film soundtracks (Ascenseur pour l'Échafaud, Siesta), the archival outtake collections Columbia assembled later (Water Babies, Directions, Circle in the Round), and posthumous vault releases (Rubberband). The early-50s Prestige 10-inch LPs are summarized via the 12-inch albums that consolidated them. The Gil Evans orchestral albums are included—they are credited to and led by Miles. See the legend for details on all exclusions.

PRESTIGE ERA (1951–1961) Bebop Roots, Hard Bop & the First Great Quintet

Album / YearKey PersonnelNotes
The New Sounds
1951		 Sonny Rollins (tenor), John Lewis (piano), Roy Haynes (drums) Prestige 10". His first album as a bandleader, recorded Oct 1951. Bebop. The start of the Prestige relationship; material later folded into 12" LPs like Dig.
Blue Haze
1956(sessions 1953–54)		 Charlie Parker (tenor!), John Lewis, Horace Silver (piano), Percy Heath (bass), Max Roach, Art Blakey, Kenny Clarke (drums) Prestige. Consolidates 10" sessions. Notable for Charlie Parker appearing on tenor sax, and the moody title blues recorded with the lights off.
Walkin'
1957(sessions 1954)		 J.J. Johnson (trombone), Lucky Thompson (tenor), Horace Silver (piano), Percy Heath (bass), Kenny Clarke (drums) Prestige. The April 1954 title track is a landmark—often cited as the recording that announced hard bop, pulling jazz back to blues and swing after cool jazz.
Bags' Groove
1957(sessions 1954)		 Sonny Rollins (tenor), Thelonious Monk (piano), Milt Jackson (vibes), Percy Heath, Kenny Clarke Prestige. Includes the famous Christmas Eve 1954 session with Monk and the Rollins date that produced "Airegin," "Oleo," and "Doxy"—all future standards.
Musings of Miles
1955		 Red Garland (piano), Oscar Pettiford (bass), Philly Joe Jones (drums) Prestige. His first 12" LP as sole leader; a quartet date previewing the rhythm-section sound of the coming quintet.
Miles: The New Miles Davis Quintet
1956		 First Great Quintet debut: John Coltrane (tenor), Red Garland (piano), Paul Chambers (bass), Philly Joe Jones (drums) Prestige. Studio debut of the First Great Quintet, formed after his career-reviving 1955 Newport appearance. Recorded at Van Gelder's Hackensack studio.
Cookin' / Relaxin' / Workin' / Steamin'
1957–1961(recorded 1956, two marathon sessions)		 First Great Quintet: Coltrane, Garland, Chambers, Philly Joe Jones Prestige. To fulfill his contract before moving to Columbia, the quintet cut four albums' worth of material in two legendary single-day sessions (May & Oct 1956)—first takes, club-set energy. Among the most celebrated small-group jazz recordings ever made.
Dig / Collectors' Items / Miles Davis and Horns
1956–57(sessions 1951–56)		 Sonny Rollins, Jackie McLean (alto), Charlie Parker, various Prestige. 12" LPs assembling his early-50s small-group sessions, documenting the bebop-to-hard-bop transition (and his years battling addiction, which he kicked in 1954).

FIRST COLUMBIA ERA (1957–1963) The Quintet/Sextet Peak & the Gil Evans Orchestral Albums

Album / YearKey PersonnelNotes
'Round About Midnight
1957		 First Great Quintet: Coltrane, Garland, Chambers, Philly Joe Jones Columbia debut. Opens with his iconic Harmon-muted reading of Monk's "'Round Midnight." The bridge from Prestige grit to Columbia polish.
Miles Ahead
1957		 Gil Evans (arranger/conductor) + 19-piece orchestra; Miles on flugelhorn Columbia. First of the great Gil Evans collaborations—Miles as sole soloist floating over Evans' impressionist brass-and-woodwind clouds. Third stream/orchestral jazz landmark.
Milestones
1958		 Sextet: Coltrane, Cannonball Adderley (alto), Garland, Chambers, Philly Joe Jones Columbia. The sextet with both Coltrane and Adderley. The title track is his first fully modal composition—the doorway to Kind of Blue.
⭐ Porgy and Bess
1959		 Gil Evans (arranger/conductor) + orchestra Columbia. Gershwin reimagined. Widely considered one of the finest orchestral jazz albums; "Summertime" with Evans' voicings is definitive.
⭐ Kind of Blue
1959		 Coltrane (tenor), Adderley (alto), Bill Evans & Wynton Kelly (piano), Chambers (bass), Jimmy Cobb (drums) Columbia. The best-selling jazz album of all time and the defining statement of modal jazz. Recorded in two sessions with minimal sketches; "So What," "Blue in Green," "All Blues," "Flamenco Sketches." Arguably the most influential jazz record ever made.
Sketches of Spain
1960		 Gil Evans (arranger/conductor) + orchestra Columbia. Spanish folk and classical themes, centered on Rodrigo's "Concierto de Aranjuez." The most ambitious Davis/Evans work; a genre unto itself.
Someday My Prince Will Come
1961		 Hank Mobley (tenor), Wynton Kelly, Chambers, Jimmy Cobb; Coltrane guests on two tracks Columbia. Transitional standards date; Coltrane's blistering cameo on the title track was his last studio appearance with Miles. Frances Taylor (Davis's wife) on the cover—his insistence on featuring Black women on his sleeves.
Seven Steps to Heaven
1963		 George Coleman (tenor), Victor Feldman/Herbie Hancock (piano), Ron Carter (bass), Frank Butler/Tony Williams (drums) Columbia. Split between LA and NY sessions—the NY side introduces 17-year-old Tony Williams, Hancock, and Carter: the future Second Great Quintet rhythm section arrives.
Quiet Nights
1964(recorded 1962–63)		 Gil Evans (arranger/conductor) + orchestra Columbia. Bossa nova-flavored final Evans collaboration, released over the pair's objections (they considered it unfinished). Miles was furious with producer Teo Macero for years.

SECOND GREAT QUINTET (1965–1968) Post-Bop's Summit

Album / YearKey PersonnelNotes
E.S.P.
1965		 Wayne Shorter (tenor), Herbie Hancock (piano), Ron Carter (bass), Tony Williams (drums) Columbia. First studio album by the complete Second Great Quintet. All-original material; elastic "time, no changes" post-bop begins here.
⭐ Miles Smiles
1967		 Second Great Quintet Columbia. Widely viewed as the quintet's peak—fierce, free-flowing, telepathic. "Footprints," "Freedom Jazz Dance," "Circle."
Sorcerer
1967		 Second Great Quintet Columbia. Dominated by Shorter's oblique compositions; Miles contributes no tunes. Abstract, simmering, and subtle.
Nefertiti
1968(recorded 1967)		 Second Great Quintet Columbia. His last fully acoustic album. The famous title track inverts jazz form—the horns repeat the melody while the rhythm section improvises.
Miles in the Sky
1968		 Second Great Quintet; George Benson guests (guitar) Columbia. First appearance of electric piano and electric guitar—the door to fusion cracks open.
Filles de Kilimanjaro
1968		 Quintet; Chick Corea (electric piano) & Dave Holland (bass) replace Hancock/Carter on two tracks Columbia. "Directions in music by Miles Davis" appears on the cover. Rock rhythms, electric keys; the quintet dissolves into the electric future. "Mademoiselle Mabry" reworks Hendrix's "The Wind Cries Mary."

ELECTRIC ERA (1969–1975) Fusion, Funk & Studio-as-Instrument

Album / YearKey PersonnelNotes
In a Silent Way
1969		 Shorter, Hancock, Corea, Joe Zawinul (keyboards), John McLaughlin (guitar), Holland, Williams; prod. Teo Macero Columbia. The serene birth of jazz fusion. Macero's tape-splicing constructs the album from studio jams—the "studio as instrument" approach begins.
⭐ Bitches Brew
1970(recorded Aug 1969)		 Shorter, Zawinul, Corea, McLaughlin, Holland, Jack DeJohnette (drums), Bennie Maupin (bass clarinet), Harvey Brooks (electric bass), Lenny White, more; prod. Teo Macero Columbia. Double-album Big Bang of fusion—dense, psychedelic, churning. Gold record; Grammy winner. Its alumni founded Weather Report, Return to Forever, Mahavishnu Orchestra, and Headhunters.
A Tribute to Jack Johnson
1971		 McLaughlin (guitar), Michael Henderson (electric bass), Billy Cobham (drums), Hancock (organ), Steve Grossman (soprano) Columbia. Lean, roaring jazz-rock built for the boxing documentary—Miles's most guitar-driven, rock-forward record. "Right Off" rides one colossal groove.
On the Corner
1972		 Henderson, McLaughlin, Corea, Hancock, Dave Liebman (sax), Badal Roy (tabla), Colin Walcott (sitar), Jack DeJohnette, more; prod. Teo Macero Columbia. Funk/Stockhausen collage aimed at young Black audiences; famously released with no musician credits. Savaged by jazz critics in 1972—now revered as a prophetic ancestor of hip-hop, electronic, and groove-based music.
Big Fun
1974(sessions 1969–72)		 Large casts incl. Shorter, Zawinul, McLaughlin, Corea, Hancock, Indian instrumentation Columbia. Double album gathering sprawling session pieces ("Great Expectations," "Go Ahead John") from the Brew-to-Corner years. Assembled by Macero, issued as a proper new release.
Get Up with It
1974(sessions 1970–74)		 Pete Cosey (guitar), Reggie Lucas (guitar), Henderson, Al Foster (drums), Mtume (percussion), more Columbia. His last studio release before retirement. Contains the 32-minute organ elegy "He Loved Him Madly" (for Duke Ellington)—a proto-ambient landmark Brian Eno cited as an influence—plus the ferocious "Rated X." Miles then withdrew from music, 1975–1980.

COMEBACK ERA (1981–1985) Return, Pop Standards & Late Columbia

Album / YearKey PersonnelNotes
The Man with the Horn
1981		 Bill Evans (sax), Mike Stern (guitar), Marcus Miller (bass), Al Foster (drums), Mino Cinelu (percussion) Columbia. The comeback after five silent years and serious health struggles. Uneven but historic; the working band it launched toured relentlessly.
Star People
1983		 Bill Evans, Stern, John Scofield (guitar), Miller, Foster, Cinelu; Gil Evans (uncredited arranging help) Columbia. Blues-centered; the last record with any Gil Evans involvement. Cover features Miles's own drawings.
Decoy
1984		 Scofield, Darryl Jones (bass), Robert Irving III (synths), Branford Marsalis (soprano), Foster, Cinelu Columbia. Synth-funk direction with Irving co-producing; Grammy winner (Best Jazz Instrumental Performance).
You're Under Arrest
1985		 Scofield, Stern, Jones, Irving, Foster; Sting cameo (French voice) Columbia. Pop covers as trumpet vehicles—Cyndi Lauper's "Time After Time" and Michael Jackson's "Human Nature" became permanent concert staples. His final Columbia studio album; he left after ~30 years.
Aura
1989(recorded 1985)		 Palle Mikkelborg (composer/arranger), Danish Radio Big Band, McLaughlin (guitar), Vince Wilburn Jr. (drums) Columbia. Orchestral suite written for Miles by Danish trumpeter Palle Mikkelborg after his Léonie Sonning Prize. Shelved for four years; won a Grammy. A late-career outlier—his "color symphony."

WARNER BROS. ERA (1986–1991) The Final Chapter

Album / YearKey PersonnelNotes
⭐ Tutu
1986		 Marcus Miller (composer/producer/nearly all instruments), George Duke, Omar Hakim, Paulinho da Costa Warner Bros. Named for Archbishop Desmond Tutu. Miller-built synth/drum-machine soundscapes with Miles as lone voice over top—a late-career reinvention. Grammy winner; the Irving Penn cover portrait is iconic.
Amandla
1989		 Marcus Miller (producer), Kenny Garrett (alto), George Duke, Joey DeFrancesco, Al Foster Warner Bros. Zouk, funk, and go-go rhythms folded into the Miller production approach; "Amandla" is a Zulu/Xhosa rallying cry for power. His last album released in his lifetime under his own leadership.
Doo-Bop
1992(recorded 1991; released posthumously)		 Easy Mo Bee (producer, raps), hip-hop production Warner Bros. His final studio project: a jazz/hip-hop fusion left incomplete at his death (Sept 28, 1991) and finished by Easy Mo Bee. Grammy winner. Even at the end, chasing the sound of the street.
Key Points About Miles Davis's Discography as a Leader:

The restless reinventor: No musician reshaped jazz more times. Miles Davis (1926–1991) stood at the center of at least five revolutions: cool jazz (the 1949–50 nonet sessions later compiled as Birth of the Cool), hard bop ("Walkin'"), modal jazz (Milestones, Kind of Blue), post-bop (the Second Great Quintet), and fusion (In a Silent Way, Bitches Brew)—before anticipating ambient, funk, electronic, and hip-hop directions in the 70s and 80s. His stated ethos: never look back.
A note on Birth of the Cool: The nonet music (with Gerry Mulligan, Lee Konitz, Gil Evans arranging) was recorded in 1949–50 as singles and only compiled as the LP Birth of the Cool in 1957—technically a compilation, so it doesn't get its own row here, but it's canonical and among the most important music he ever led.
The Prestige years & the 10-inch problem (1951–1956): His early Prestige output appeared as 10-inch LPs (The New Sounds, Young Man with a Horn, Blue Period, etc.) that the label later recombined into 12-inch albums (Dig, Blue Haze, Walkin', Bags' Groove, Collectors' Items)—so "album count" in this era is inherently fuzzy. This report presents the standard 12-inch canon. The era ends with the legendary two-day marathon that produced Cookin', Relaxin', Workin', and Steamin'.
The two Great Quintets: The First Great Quintet (1955–58: Coltrane, Red Garland, Paul Chambers, Philly Joe Jones; expanded to a sextet with Cannonball Adderley) defined small-group hard bop and peaked with Kind of Blue's sextet. The Second Great Quintet (1964–68: Wayne Shorter, Herbie Hancock, Ron Carter, Tony Williams) redefined group interplay with "time, no changes" freedom—E.S.P. through Filles de Kilimanjaro may be the most influential run of small-group jazz records ever made.
Gil Evans: Arranger Gil Evans was Davis's closest musical partner for over 30 years. Their orchestral albums—Miles Ahead (1957), Porgy and Bess (1959), Sketches of Spain (1960), and the disowned Quiet Nights (1964)—are included here since they are Miles Davis albums by billing and leadership; Evans also contributed quietly to many other projects through Star People.
Teo Macero and the studio as instrument: From In a Silent Way onward, producer Teo Macero assembled Davis's electric albums from hours of open-ended session tape via razor-blade editing, loops, and effects—a radical, hip-hop-anticipating method. This is why several 70s "studio albums" (Big Fun, Get Up with It) blur the line between session and compilation; both were released as new albums and are included, while the purely archival collections Columbia issued later (Water Babies, Directions, Circle in the Round) are not.
The sideman academy: Davis's bands were the greatest finishing school in jazz. Alumni who became leaders include John Coltrane, Cannonball Adderley, Bill Evans, Wayne Shorter, Herbie Hancock, Ron Carter, Tony Williams, Chick Corea, Joe Zawinul, John McLaughlin, Jack DeJohnette, Dave Holland, Keith Jarrett, John Scofield, Marcus Miller, and Kenny Garrett—founders of Weather Report, Return to Forever, Mahavishnu Orchestra, and the Headhunters among them.
Exclusions, for the record: Live albums (My Funny Valentine, At Fillmore, Agharta, Pangaea, We Want Miles, etc.); co-billed dates (Miles Davis and Milt Jackson, Blue Moods with Mingus's Debut label, Legrand Jazz); soundtracks (Ascenseur pour l'Échafaud, 1958—improvised to the film reels in one night and hugely influential; Siesta, 1987, with Marcus Miller); the final collaboration Miles & Quincy Live at Montreux (1993); and posthumous vault constructions (Rubberband, 2019).
